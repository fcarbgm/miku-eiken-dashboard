Warm-Up Stories

模擬試験1で間違えた 全25問 の語彙・表現を使った、10本の短い話。
おかしな話ですが、英語としては全部ふつうです。ここに出てくる語は、すべて自然な使い方・自然な組み合わせで使われています。そのまま覚えて、そのまま使えます。各話に 直訳（英語の語順のまま） つき。

HOW TO USE ― パパへ
ラウンド1： 1文ずつ、自然な速さで読む。Miku がそのまま繰り返す。止まってもやり直させない。最後まで通す。
ラウンド2： 直訳を見ながらもう一度。英語の順番と日本語の順番のズレを目で確認させる。
ラウンド3： 2文ずつまとめて読む。繰り返させる。
ラウンド4（チャレンジ）： 1本まるごと読む。Miku が記憶だけで再現する。7割言えたら合格。
所要時間：1本 約4分。毎日2〜3本。

まず読んで ― 英語は「言い切ってから、後ろに足す」

日本語と英語は、情報を出す順番が正反対です。ここが、リスニングで置いていかれる本当の理由です。

日本語：「歯医者は / 私に / 四十年分の知恵を / 授けようと / した」→ 動詞が最後。最後まで聞かないと何をしたか分からない。
English: She / tried / to impart / forty years of dental wisdom / to me.
→ 彼女は / 試みた / 授けることを / 四十年分の歯科の知恵を / 私に　2語目で「試みた」と分かる。あとは後ろに足すだけ。

英語が「後ろに足す」6つの型 ― 全部この10話に入っています

型形例（このお話から）
①名詞 ＋ ~ing / 過去分詞（後ろから名詞を説明）a painting of a glacier finished from memory
②名詞 ＋ that / which / whoa chart that matched nothing on a modern map
③, which …（文全体に説明を足す）…, which gave them objective data
④resulting in / leading to（結果を後ろに足す）it will be swallowed, resulting in the destruction of both stars
⑤rather than … / not because …（後ろから否定で訂正）deliberately torn down rather than left to fall into disrepair
⑥前置詞が語を決めるslip away to ／ drop out of ／ attest to
直訳の読み方： 「/」は英語が意味のカタマリを出した順番です。日本語として不自然でかまいません。その不自然さが、英語と日本語のズレの正体です。

THE TEN STORIES

STORY 1My Dentist Explains Everything ― 説明の長い歯医者

My dentist has a diagram of every tooth in my mouth taped to the ceiling, so I have something to read while she works. Last month she found a cavity so deep that she had no choice but to extract the tooth. Before she began, she tried to impart forty years of dental wisdom to me, which took longer than the procedure itself. She offered to implant a titanium screw in the gap, and I politely declined. She also warned me that a vitamin deficiency was slowing my recovery, so I now take supplements every morning. On the drive home I was given a traffic citation for speeding in a school zone. When I told her, she said I was better off taking the bus. Her advice was excellent. Her bill did not add up.

STORY 2The Scholarship Committee ― 奨学金の選考委員会

Every year this committee selects one scholarship recipient from about two hundred applicants. Our chairman retired last spring and I am his successor, which means the final decision is now mine. This March a student arrived with a portfolio, a referral from her art teacher, and a painting of a glacier she had finished entirely from memory. Her personal essay ran to eleven pages, so we asked her to abbreviate it. She cut it to four, but it was still redundant, because the second half simply repeated the first. When we asked her to attest to her grades, she admitted that she had fabricated two of them. We rejected her, and the small gallery that signed her a year later nearly went under before her work began to sell. She has flourished ever since, and I have never been forgiven.

STORY 3Seat 24B ― 飛行機の 24B

Because turbulence was expected over the mountains, the captain turned on the seat belt sign an hour early. There was so much distortion on the intercom that nobody understood a single word of his announcement. The man beside me had left the strap of his bag slack, and it slid the whole length of the aisle. He tried to slip away to the back of the plane to retrieve it, and a flight attendant sent him straight back to his seat. He told me he had dropped out of engineering school because he could never sit still for ninety minutes. The delay ate up the entire afternoon and we landed long after dark. He was remarkably generous about the whole thing. I was not.

STORY 4The Sleep Study ― 睡眠実験

My brother volunteered for a sleep study last winter. Every morning the researchers gave him a survey asking how rested he felt, but they admitted that this kind of self-report is too subjective to rely on. So they also attached a small disposable sensor to his forehead to record his brain waves, which gave them objective data. The sensor was so light that he forgot he was wearing it, unlike the older equipment, which was heavy, uncomfortable, and covered in electrical wires. The team eventually hopes to build a version that covers the entire head and produces more comprehensive data. My brother doubts the study will ever be published, although I suspect it will. He says he slept badly for six weeks and received a T-shirt. The T-shirt, he admits, is objectively excellent.

STORY 5The Largest Jigsaw Puzzle in Britain ― ローマ時代の壁画

A construction crew in London struck something hard last spring and called in the archaeologists. In a pit beneath the site they recovered several thousand fragments of painted Roman wall plaster. Because the pieces had been buried together rather than scattered across the ground, the team concluded that the building had been deliberately torn down rather than left to fall into disrepair. The paintings used a great deal of yellow pigment, which was expensive and uncommon at the time, and this points to the prosperity of the owner. The surrounding neighbourhood appears to have been affluent. Archaeologists speculate that the house belonged to a wealthy merchant, though nobody can prove it. The excavation and reassembly took four years. It is the largest jigsaw puzzle in Britain, and the box lid is missing.

STORY 6Next Tuesday ― ベテルギウスの伴星

Astronomers suspected for almost a century that Betelgeuse had a companion star, but most of them doubted that it would ever be confirmed. Betelgeuse is so large and so bright that the smaller star was lost in its glare, and everything written about it remained pure conjecture. A new technique took thousands of extremely short exposures, which reduced the blur caused by our atmosphere, and at last the companion appeared. It is small, hot and blue-white, while Betelgeuse is enormous, cool and red. The companion's orbit brings it extremely close, and within the next ten thousand years it will be swallowed, resulting in the destruction of both stars. Ten thousand years sounds like a comfortable distance away. To an astronomer it is next Tuesday. Nobody has yet agreed on a name for it.

STORY 7The Seal That Was Not a Seal ― サメとおとり

Many deep-sea animals emit a faint light from their undersides in order to disguise themselves against the brighter water above. Seen from below they simply disappear, which makes life very difficult for any predator that hunts by silhouette. Researchers in Australia wondered whether people could borrow the same trick, since a surfer viewed from below looks a great deal like a seal, and a seal is a great white's natural prey. They built seal-shaped decoys, fitted LED strips along the bottom of each one, and towed them behind a boat. Mimicking nature worked: the lit decoys were attacked far less often than the dark ones. The study suggests that lights fitted to a surfboard might one day reduce attacks on swimmers. So far no surfer has volunteered to test this personally. The sharks have declined to comment.

STORY 8Reading the Chart Correctly ― トゥパイアの海図

Tupaia was born on Raiatea and was forced to flee when warriors from a neighbouring island invaded his homeland. He settled on Tahiti, where he became both a respected priest and an expert navigator. When Captain Cook arrived in 1768, Tupaia joined the expedition as an interpreter and guide, and during the voyage he drew a chart showing more than seventy islands. The chart puzzled historians for two centuries, because it matched nothing on a modern map. Two German professors have now argued that everyone had been reading it from the wrong perspective. Tupaia had drawn the islands in relation to one another, not according to fixed geographical position. Read that way, the chart turns out to be remarkably accurate. Historians spent two hundred years concluding that he was confused. He was simply not European.

STORY 9My Parents' Anniversary ― 両親の記念日

I telephoned a restaurant on Friday to book a table for my parents' anniversary. The woman told me they had tables at six thirty or at eight, and nothing in between. STORY 10Not Really ― 新しい職場

A friend asked me last week whether my new job was hectic. Not really — the workload is actually lighter than it was at my old company.
