1. Should AI be used in education?
Society & Technology

🎙 Script (約350語 ≒ 3分)

Good afternoon. Today, I would like to talk about a question that affects every student in Japan: should AI be used in education? There's no denying that AI has already changed the way we live, and now it is entering our classrooms. In my opinion, AI should be used in education, but carefully.

First, let me explain the benefits. AI tutors are available twenty-four hours a day, so students can study whenever they want. In the past, only wealthy families could afford private tutors. Now, thanks to AI, students from low-income backgrounds can receive the same support as everyone else. In other words, AI makes education more equal. Moreover, AI can analyze each student's weak points and offer personalized lessons. Schools that fail to keep pace with this technology may leave their students behind.

However, while it's true that AI is convenient, it is not without risks. If students depend on AI for every answer, they may stop thinking for themselves. Their creativity and problem-solving skills could become weaker, not stronger. Furthermore, AI systems collect huge amounts of personal data, which may infringe on students' privacy. Teachers also worry that their role could become obsolete, although I believe technology can never replace human encouragement.

So, what is the answer? I believe AI is a double-edged sword. Used wisely, it cuts through inequality; used carelessly, it cuts away our ability to think. Therefore, schools should use AI as a supporting tool, not as a replacement for teachers. The more we understand its limits, the more safely we can enjoy its benefits.

In conclusion, AI should be welcomed into education — but with clear rules. If we combine the efficiency of machines with the warmth of human teachers, we can get the best of both worlds. Thank you very much.

💬 Interviewer Q&A — 想定質問と理想回答

Q1. Do you think AI will replace teachers completely in the future?
No, I don't think so. While it's true that AI can deliver information efficiently, teachers do much more than that — they motivate students and understand their feelings. I believe the teacher's role will change, but it will never become obsolete.
Q2. Have you ever used AI for studying? How was it?
Yes, I use an AI tutor to practice English pronunciation. It points out my mistakes immediately, so I can correct them on the spot. However, I still need my human teacher to explain why something is wrong, so I use both.
Q3. Some people say AI makes students lazy. What do you think?
I partly agree. If students simply copy AI answers, their thinking skills will decline. However, it comes down to how we use it — a calculator doesn't make mathematicians lazy, and AI won't make good students lazy either.
Q4. What rules should schools make about AI?
I would suggest three rules: students must protect their personal data, they must not submit AI writing as their own, and they should always double-check AI information, because it doesn't always add up.

2. Does advertising have too much influence on how we spend money?
Society & Technology ｜ 2026年第1回 エッセイ問題そのもの！

🎙 Script (約350語 ≒ 3分)

Hello, everyone. Have you ever bought something you didn't really need? I have — many times! Today I want to discuss whether advertising has too much influence on the way people spend money. My answer is yes, it does, especially in the age of social media.

To begin with, advertising today is not what it used to be. In the past, we saw the same TV commercials as everyone else. Now, companies collect our personal data and show us advertisements that are designed just for us. Every time we search for something online, that information is recorded. As a result, we see the perfect advertisement at the perfect moment — and we can hardly resist it. This is not a coincidence; it is a carefully calculated strategy.

Moreover, advertisements often create desires rather than satisfy needs. Influencers make luxury goods look like daily necessities, and many young people end up spending money they cannot afford, simply to keep up with trends. In the worst cases, they live off credit and fall into debt. There is also the problem of reliable information: some advertisements exaggerate or even fabricate the effects of a product, so consumers cannot make informed decisions.

On the other hand, it would be unfair to say advertising is all bad. It supports the economy, funds free services like search engines, and introduces us to genuinely useful products. Without advertising, many small businesses would never reach their customers.

Nevertheless, when I weigh the pros and cons, I believe the influence has become too strong, because the balance of power is unfair: companies know everything about us, but we know almost nothing about their methods. Therefore, we should teach media literacy at school and require companies to be transparent about targeted advertising.

In conclusion, advertising is a clever servant but a dangerous master. We cannot escape it, but we can learn to see through it. Thank you very much.

💬 Interviewer Q&A

Q1. Have you ever bought something because of an advertisement and regretted it?
Yes. Last year I bought a face cream because an influencer praised it, but it didn't work at all. The advertisement clearly exaggerated its effects. Since then, I always check independent reviews before buying — the claims in ads don't always add up.
Q2. Should advertising to children be restricted?
Definitely. Children cannot tell the difference between information and persuasion, so advertising to them is like a game with one-sided rules. Many European countries already restrict it, and I think Japan should follow suit.
Q3. How can consumers protect themselves from targeted ads?
First, we can limit the data we share by changing privacy settings. Second, we should wait twenty-four hours before buying anything expensive. If we still want it the next day, it may be a real need rather than a desire created by an ad.
Q4. Isn't advertising necessary for the economy?
While it's true that the economy depends on consumption, an economy built on debt and impulse buying is not healthy in the long run. I believe honest advertising can support the economy without manipulating consumers.

3. Should self-driving cars be allowed on public roads?
Society & Technology ｜ 2025年第3回 エッセイ問題そのもの！

🎙 Script (約350語 ≒ 3分)

Good afternoon. Imagine getting into a car, saying your destination, and simply relaxing while the car drives itself. This is no longer science fiction. Today I will discuss whether self-driving cars should be allowed on public roads. My position is yes — under strict conditions.

The strongest argument is safety. It is said that over ninety percent of traffic accidents are caused by human error: drivers who are tired, distracted, or careless. Machines, on the other hand, never get sleepy and never check their phones. If self-driving technology can reduce accidents even by half, thousands of lives will be saved every year. In an aging society like Japan, these cars could also give elderly people the freedom to travel without depending on their families. When it comes to convenience, the benefits are enormous.

However, opponents raise serious concerns, and rightly so. First, there is the question of responsibility: if a self-driving car causes an accident, who should be blamed — the passenger, the carmaker, or the software company? Until this question is answered clearly, full permission would be premature. We cannot rule out the possibility of system failures, either. A computer that freezes on the highway could cause a terrible collision. Second, millions of taxi and truck drivers might lose their jobs, so the change could come at the expense of workers.

Considering both sides, I believe we should allow self-driving cars step by step: first in limited areas at low speeds, then gradually expand as the technology proves itself. This is exactly how Japan introduced other new technologies, and it worked.

In conclusion, self-driving cars should be allowed on public roads, but only with clear laws about responsibility and a gradual introduction. If we prepare carefully, this technology will not only make our roads safer but also make our society kinder to the elderly. Thank you very much.

💬 Interviewer Q&A

Q1. Would you personally ride in a self-driving car?
Honestly, I would feel nervous at first, but yes. I already trust machines in other ways — elevators and airplanes mostly fly on autopilot. Once the technology has a proven safety record, I think my fear will fade with time.
Q2. What should happen to drivers who lose their jobs?
The government and companies should offer retraining programs so drivers can take on new roles, such as monitoring the vehicles remotely. Change is inevitable, but leaving workers behind is a choice we don't have to make.
Q3. Who do you think should be responsible when a self-driving car has an accident?
In my opinion, the manufacturer should bear the main responsibility, because the passenger cannot control the car. However, owners should still be responsible for proper maintenance. A clear law must decide this before the cars spread widely.
Q4. Do you think self-driving cars will be common in Japan in twenty years?
Yes, especially in rural areas where bus services are disappearing and the population is aging. Necessity drives change, and Japan's shrinking workforce means we will need this technology sooner rather than later.

4. Is social media doing more harm than good?
Society & Technology

🎙 Script (約350語 ≒ 3分)

Hello, everyone. I am a high school student, which means I belong to the first generation that has never known a world without social media. Today I want to answer a difficult question: is social media doing more harm than good? After thinking about it deeply, my answer is that the harm is winning — for now.

Let me start with the good points, because there are many. Social media connects people who would otherwise never meet. It gives ordinary people a voice, helps small businesses grow, and spreads news faster than any newspaper. During disasters, it saves lives by sharing information instantly. There's no denying that these benefits are real.

However, the costs are becoming too heavy to ignore. First, social media is designed to be addictive. Companies earn money from our attention, so their apps are built to eat up as much of our time as possible. Many students, including my friends, check their phones hundreds of times a day and find it hard to concentrate on studying. Second, it damages mental health. Everyone posts only their happiest moments, so we constantly compare our ordinary lives with others' highlights. This comparison leads to anxiety and low self-esteem, especially among teenagers. Third, false information spreads six times faster than the truth, which makes it difficult for society to agree on basic facts.

While it's true that we cannot turn back the clock, we are not powerless either. We can set time limits, turn off notifications, and follow accounts that inspire us instead of accounts that make us feel inferior. Schools should also teach students how these apps are designed to capture our attention.

In conclusion, social media is currently doing more harm than good, but the problem is not the tool itself — it is how we use it. If we become smarter users, the balance can shift. The choice, ultimately, lies in our hands. Thank you very much.

💬 Interviewer Q&A

Q1. How much time do you spend on social media, and do you think it's too much?
I used to spend about three hours a day, but I realized it was eating up my study time. Now I have switched my phone to English-only media, so even my scrolling time supports my learning goals.
Q2. Should the government regulate social media companies?
To some extent, yes. I don't want the government to control what people say, but companies should be required to protect children's data and to label false information. Freedom of speech and freedom to manipulate are two different things.
Q3. Why do you think teenagers are especially affected?
Teenagers are still forming their identities, so they care deeply about others' opinions. When "likes" become a number that measures your value, every post feels like a test. Adults have more life experience to fall back on, but we don't yet.
Q4. Can social media ever be good for studying?
Absolutely. I follow English-learning accounts, and short videos taught me many natural expressions. The key is to use social media on purpose, with a goal — not just to kill time.

📚 スタディガイド（日本語）

5. Should governments do more to protect endangered species?
Environment ｜ 2025年第3回 要約問題（biologgers）と直結

🎙 Script (約350語 ≒ 3分)

Good afternoon. Every day, dozens of species disappear from our planet forever. Today I would like to discuss whether governments should do more to protect endangered species. My answer is a strong yes, and I will explain why this is not just an animal problem, but a human problem.

First, we must understand that nature is a system in which everything is connected. When one species disappears, the effect spreads like falling dominoes. For example, if bees continue to decline, many crops cannot grow, and our food supply will be directly threatened. Protecting endangered species is therefore not charity — it is self-defense. In the long run, the cost of doing nothing will be far greater than the cost of action.

Second, technology now makes protection more effective than ever. Scientists attach small electronic devices called biologgers to animals, which allow researchers to gather data about their heart rates, movements, and even the pollution levels of their environments. Thanks to such tools, governments can identify exactly where animals are in danger and take action before it is too late. However, this research needs money, and only governments can provide it on a large scale.

Of course, some people argue that protecting the economy should come first, and that strict environmental rules hurt farmers and companies. While it's true that there is sometimes a conflict, the choice between the economy and nature is often a false one. Eco-tourism, for instance, creates jobs precisely because rare animals survive. A dead forest earns nothing forever; a living forest earns money every year.

In conclusion, governments should definitely do more: stronger laws, more funding for research, and better protection of natural habitats. If we act now, future generations will inherit a rich planet; if we delay, they will inherit a silent one. The animals cannot speak at elections — so we must speak for them. Thank you very much.

💬 Interviewer Q&A

Q1. Is there an endangered species in Japan that you care about?
Yes, the Japanese crested ibis, or toki. It once disappeared completely from the wild in Japan, but thanks to a government breeding program, it is now returning to Sado Island. It proves that action works when we don't give up.
Q2. Some say attaching devices to animals stresses them. What do you think?
That's a fair concern — stressed animals may behave differently, so the data may not be reliable. Researchers should make the devices smaller and lighter, and weigh the benefits against the risks for each species.
Q3. What can ordinary people do to help?
More than we think. We can avoid products that destroy habitats, support conservation groups, and choose eco-friendly tourism. Small choices add up when millions of people make them together.
Q4. Should developing countries be asked to protect nature even if they are poor?
Rich countries should not simply demand it — they should pay for it, because they caused much of the damage while becoming rich. International funds that reward forest protection are a step in the right direction.

📚 スタディガイド（日本語）

文法ポイント

② 対句（parallelism）：If we act now, ... a rich planet; if we delay, ... a silent one. 同じ形を繰り返すと記憶にも採点者にも強く残る。
③ 過去分詞の後置修飾：devices called biologgers「バイオロガーと呼ばれる装置」。

6. Should Japan rely more on renewable energy?
Environment

🎙 Script (約350語 ≒ 3分)

Hello, everyone. Japan imports almost ninety percent of its energy from other countries. Today I would like to discuss whether Japan should rely more on renewable energy, such as solar, wind, and geothermal power. My answer is yes — not only for the environment, but for our security and our economy.

First, consider our current situation. Because Japan depends heavily on imported oil and gas, our electricity prices rise whenever there is a crisis somewhere in the world. Renewable energy, by contrast, is domestic: the sun and the wind cannot be stopped by international conflicts. If we increased our use of renewables, we would become far less vulnerable to events beyond our control. Energy independence is national security.

Second, the environmental argument is clear. Japan has promised to reduce carbon emissions to virtually zero by 2050. There is no way to keep this promise while burning fossil fuels. Moreover, Japan is a volcanic country with enormous geothermal potential, and as an island nation, we are surrounded by wind. In other words, nature has already given us the resources; we simply need the will to use them.

Of course, critics point out real problems. Solar panels do not work at night, wind is unpredictable, and building new systems is expensive. Some also worry that beautiful landscapes could be damaged. These concerns should not be brushed aside. However, battery technology is improving every year, costs have fallen dramatically over the past decade, and careful planning can protect scenery. The problems are shrinking while the benefits are growing.

Taking everything into consideration, I believe Japan should invest boldly in renewable energy. It will clean our air, stabilize our prices, create new industries, and free us from dependence on other nations. What we need is not new technology but new determination. The energy of the future is already shining above us and blowing around us — we just have to catch it. Thank you very much.

💬 Interviewer Q&A

Q1. Which renewable energy source is best for Japan?
I would say geothermal power, because Japan has the world's third-largest geothermal resources, and unlike solar, it works twenty-four hours a day in all weather. Iceland already lives off geothermal energy — Japan could learn from its example.
Q2. What about nuclear power? Is it a good alternative?
That's a difficult question for Japan after 2011. Nuclear power produces no carbon emissions, but the risks and the waste problem remain unsolved. I think it may be necessary for the time being, but renewables should be our final goal.
Q3. Can ordinary households contribute to this change?
Yes. Families can install solar panels, choose electricity companies that use renewables, and simply waste less energy. Government subsidies would encourage more people to take the first step.
Q4. Won't electricity become more expensive if we switch?
In the short term, possibly. But in the long run, sunlight and wind are free, while fossil fuel prices keep rising. The real question is whether we pay a little more now or much more later.

📚 スタディガイド（日本語）

② whenever（複合関係詞）：whenever there is a crisis「危機があるたびに」。
③ What we need is ～（強調構文）：What we need is not new technology but new determination.「必要なのは〜だ」＋ not A but B。

7. Who should take responsibility for fighting climate change — individuals or governments?
Environment ｜ 2025年第2回 要約問題（野焼き規制）の対立構造を活用

🎙 Script (約350語 ≒ 3分)

Good afternoon. When we talk about climate change, we often hear two opposite opinions. Some say, "Governments must solve it." Others say, "Change begins with each of us." Today I will argue that both are necessary, but government action must come first — and I will explain why with a simple example.

This is where governments must step in. Only governments can build charging stations, invest in clean public transportation, and support farmers with subsidies so that eco-friendly choices become affordable choices. Only governments can make laws that stop companies from polluting. To put it simply, individuals decide what to choose, but governments decide what choices exist.

However, this does not mean we can sit back and do nothing. Governments respond to voters, and companies respond to customers. When millions of people choose eco-friendly products, save energy, and speak up, leaders have no choice but to listen. Our small actions may not cool the planet directly, but they create the political pressure that forces big actions. It is the combination, not the competition, of the two that works.

In conclusion, asking "individuals or governments?" is like asking which wing a bird needs. Governments must build the system; individuals must move within it and demand better. 14. Which is better: living in a city or living in the countryside?
Health & Lifestyle ｜ 2025年第2回 エッセイ（都市の公共交通）の語彙を活用

🎙 Script (約350語 ≒ 3分)

Hello, everyone. City or countryside — it is one of the oldest debates in the world, and today it is my turn to answer it. Since I have lived in a smaller city and visited big ones, I will compare them honestly. My conclusion is that the countryside is better for a healthy life, though the city wins in other ways.

Let me be fair to cities first. When it comes to convenience, cities are unbeatable. Trains come every three minutes, hospitals and universities are close, and there are endless opportunities for jobs and entertainment. Everything you want is within walking distance or a short ride away. For ambitious young people, the city is where careers are made.

However, this convenience comes at a price — literally and physically. Rent in central Tokyo can be three times higher than in a regional city, so people accept long commutes on trains so crowded that station staff once had to push passengers inside. Noise, pollution, and competition create constant stress. It is no wonder that many city dwellers report feeling tired all the time. In contrast, the countryside offers spacious homes, clean air, fresh local food, and a slower rhythm. Nature itself is medicine: research shows that simply spending time in forests lowers stress hormones. Neighbors know each other, and children grow up with room to play.

Of course, rural life has real weaknesses — fewer jobs, fewer hospitals, and weak public transportation, especially for young people and the elderly. But here is the interesting part: technology is closing the gap. Remote work brings city salaries to country towns, online shopping delivers anything anywhere, and online lessons connect students to teachers across the world — my own English lessons are proof.

In conclusion, the city offers opportunity, but the countryside offers health, space, and community — and thanks to technology, we no longer have to choose completely. The wisest life may be one with rural roots and global connections. That is exactly the life I hope to build. Thank you very much.

💬 Interviewer Q&A

Q1. Where would you like to live in the future?
I would like to keep my base in Kyushu but work with people around the world online. My English lessons already connect me to a teacher overseas, so I know this lifestyle is possible. I want local comfort and global opportunity together.
Q2. Should city people be required to use public transportation more?
Requiring it sounds too strong, but encouraging it makes sense — better services, cheaper passes, and car-free zones in city centers. In cities, trains are faster and cleaner than cars anyway, so good design can persuade people without forcing them.
Q3. Why are young people leaving the countryside?
Mainly for universities and jobs — the two things rural areas lack most. To bring them back, towns need remote-work infrastructure and support for young families. Some towns already offer cheap houses to newcomers, and it is working.
Q4. Is country life boring for teenagers?
Sometimes, honestly! There are fewer shops and events. But boredom has a hidden benefit: it pushes us to create our own fun and focus on goals. Some of my best studying happens because there is nothing else to do!

15. Are traditional customs worth preserving in modern Japan?
Culture & Values

🎙 Script (約350語 ≒ 3分)

Good afternoon. Japan is a country where robots serve coffee across the street from shrines built a thousand years ago. Today I would like to discuss whether traditional customs are worth preserving in modern Japan. My answer is yes — but we must preserve them as living things, not museum pieces.

First, why do traditions matter? They are our identity. Festivals, tea ceremony, calligraphy, and seasonal customs connect us to the generations who came before us. When I visit my ancestors' graves at Obon, I feel part of a story much larger than myself. In a globalized world where every city has the same shops and the same apps, traditions tell us who we are and where we come from. Without them, we would be culturally homeless.

Traditions are also treasures in a practical sense. Millions of tourists visit Japan precisely because our culture is unique. Traditional crafts support local economies, and values learned through customs — respect, patience, gratitude — carry over into daily life. Even modern Japanese strengths, like attention to detail, have their roots in traditional training.

However, we must be honest: some traditions struggle to survive, and not every custom deserves to. Some are expensive, some exclude women or young people, and some simply no longer fit modern life. Craftsmen are aging without successors, and young people find some ceremonies boring because they were never taught the meaning behind them. Herein lies the key: traditions survive when they adapt. The festivals that welcome newcomers and use social media are flourishing; the ones that refuse all change are dying. Tradition, after all, was innovation once.

In conclusion, traditional customs are absolutely worth preserving — by keeping their spirit while updating their form. We should teach the meaning, not just the manners; invite everyone, not just insiders. If each generation adds one new page instead of just protecting old ones, Japanese culture will never end. It will simply continue — as it always has. Thank you very much.

💬 Interviewer Q&A

Q1. Which Japanese tradition is most important to you personally?
Obon. My family visits our ancestors' graves together, and my grandmother tells stories about relatives I never met. It taught me that family means more than the people alive today — it is a chain, and I am one link.
Q2. Should schools spend more time teaching traditional culture?
Yes, but through experience, not textbooks. One real tea ceremony teaches more than ten pages about it. If students feel the atmosphere — the silence, the respect — the interest follows naturally.
Q3. Is globalization a threat to Japanese culture?
It is both a threat and an opportunity. Yes, some customs fade as lifestyles change. But globalization also gave Japanese culture its biggest audience in history — anime fans overseas now study calligraphy and visit shrines. Culture that travels, survives.
Q4. If a tradition excludes some people, should it change or be protected as it is?
I believe it should change. The heart of a tradition is its meaning, not its rules. If opening a festival to women or foreigners keeps it alive for another hundred years, that change honors the tradition more than extinction would.
